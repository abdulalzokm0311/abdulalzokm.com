## The problem I was handed

RBC's partnership ecosystem is a real edge. It is hard for other banks to copy,
and the people who engage with it stay more loyal and spend more over time. The
problem was that almost none of that showed up in the digital experience. Four
things were going wrong at once.

So the business case was clear. What was not clear was how to build it. I was
given a big, empty space, two goals that could easily fight each other, a
partner model that confuses almost everyone, three different audiences, and a
stack of brand rules. No research came with it.

Five months, one designer, from nothing.

## Making sense of it

I came into this with no prior knowledge of any of it, which turned out to be
useful. I kept asking the dumb version of the question. If I landed here knowing
nothing, what would I actually need to see, and in what order?

Nothing was handed to me, so I wrote the framing myself: the goals, who this is
for, what each of them should feel and do, and the questions worth designing
against. It is the closest thing this project had to a brief, and everything
after it came out of this board.

It named three audiences: people new to the bank, existing RBC customers
without a credit card, and current cardholders. Three audiences, but not three
paths, and working out which of them collapse together is most of what the page
design ended up being.

The first thing I had to untangle was the two goals. The Hub exists to do two
things, and they are not equal.

The obvious move is to put both in front of everyone. I talked myself out of it
fast. Two goals competing for attention on one page is how you lose both. So I
set a rule for the whole design and stuck to it.

<Principle>Give the user one thing at a time.</Principle>

## The flow

Three audiences, two paths. The first two both need the card runway, whether
they are new to RBC or already bank there without a card, so on the page they
are one person: someone with no RBC card yet. The third already has the card
and just wants to link it. Same page, completely different needs. Push both
down one path and neither gets served.

So I let them self-identify in the banner, with two CTAs.

Under that, the page runs in a deliberate order. Cards and card benefits first,
because acquisition is the priority. Then linking follows naturally: why to
link, what you get, more about the partner. One page, two clean paths, nobody's
attention split.

## When I realized it had to be a system

Somewhere in the wireframes it clicked that I was not designing pages. This is
a multi-year roadmap. Whoever comes after me adds more partners. If I designed
four nice one-off pages, they would start from scratch, and the whole thing
would drift out of consistency within a phase.

So I stopped designing pages and designed a template. It defines the sections a
partner page has, the information architecture, the hierarchy, and the flow.
Every partner page is built from it. It flexes in one main way, by partner type.

To pull this off I had to extend RBC's design system to cover things it did not
have yet. Then I brought those additions into RBC's visual language so they
became part of the system itself. The Hub did not just consume the design
system. It grew it.

## Where my first instinct was wrong

The hardest thing in this whole space is the difference between a co-brand and
a linked loyalty partner. It is the single most confusing idea here, and if a
user does not get it, they do not understand what they are being offered.

would have been easy to lean on it too hard and let the Hub start looking like

letting Avion feel like Avion was a constant small negotiation, and getting that
balance right is a lot of what makes the pages hold together.

## The partner pages

Phase one covered Petro-Canada, Moi Rewards, More Rewards and Triangle Rewards.
All four are built from the same template, so the interesting thing is not any
one of them. It is that you can move between them and the page keeps its shape
while the brand changes underneath it. That is the whole point of a template.
They should read as siblings, not as one-offs.

Petro-Canada and Moi are co-brand partners, so they lead with the card. More
Rewards and Triangle are linked loyalty, so they get to linking sooner. Same
sections, same hierarchy, different runway.

## Where it landed

At the end of my time at RBC, phase one was essentially done and handed over,
with only minor edits left, mostly visual assets I was waiting on from Avion and
small copy changes.

What I can say honestly is what I left behind. Not four pages, but a template
that is now part of RBC's design system, a flow that gives each user one thing
at a time, and a way to make a genuinely confusing partner model easy to see.
The next phase builds on that instead of starting over.

Once it is live, the goals give me clear things to watch, and honestly the
things I would most want to pressure-test.

- **Card applications through the Hub.** The acquisition goal, and the one the whole page order is built around.
- **Card-to-partner linking.** The secondary goal. Worth watching against applications rather than on its own, since the design deliberately puts it second.
- **Whether the CTAs route people.** The self-identification banner only works if the two audiences actually take the paths I built for them.
- **Whether the card art reads.** The category page fix made the co-brand distinction obvious to me. That is not the same as obvious to a first-time visitor.

## What I would take from this

I was handed ambiguity, two competing goals, a confusing model, three audiences,
a wall of brand rules, and no research. The work was turning that into something
a person could move through without thinking. I did it by building a system
instead of pages, a flow that never splits attention, and a design choice that
made the hardest idea visible.

Phase one is what I designed. The system is what I left.
