## The problem

Booking a flight is something most people do once or twice a year. That gap
matters. You don't build muscle memory for a task you repeat annually, so every
booking starts close to cold. WestJet's flow assumed more familiarity than an
occasional traveller actually has.

Three things kept coming up. Fare options were hard to compare side by side.
The search results screen carried too much at once. And the point where a
booking became final was easy to miss.

## Who this is for

I focused on the infrequent traveller. Someone booking a trip for themselves or
a family, comparing a handful of options, sensitive to price but not fluent in
fare-class language.

That framing set the bar: a person should be able to search, compare and book
without asking anyone for help, on their first attempt.

## What I changed

**Fare comparison.** Options now sit in a single scannable row with the
differences pulled forward, so the choice is between four visible things rather
than four blocks of text.

**Search results.** I cut the information on each result down to what people
said they used to decide, and moved the rest behind a deliberate expand.

**Checkout.** The final confirmation step now reads as a distinct moment, with
the total, the itinerary and the commitment all on one screen.

## Round two

The second round tested the redesign against the same tasks.

Five out of five participants searched a round-trip flight successfully. Five
out of five completed a booking end to end. Five out of five compared fare
options without hesitating on what the differences were.
