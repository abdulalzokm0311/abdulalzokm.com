## The ask

A marketing manager came to our team with the business credit cards home page
and two of its product pages. They were old and they were not working.

Everything was text. The pages made you read instead of letting you scan, and
the order of the content did not match what a business owner came to do. Two
of us took it from competitive analysis through to final hi-fi.

## The pages made you work for it

The home page opened with a wall of copy and never let up. To reach the actual
cards you had to scroll past secondary tools and lower-priority content first.

That is the whole problem in one line. The old running order:

Exploration sat third, on a page whose main job is helping someone find the
right card.

The page also served two audiences at once, business and commercial, so
neither got a clean path through. The product pages had the same habit: a
large intro paragraph almost nobody would read, then who the card was for,
then a long block of feature text, then a call to action at the bottom. The
features were worth knowing. They were just buried in prose.

## What everyone else was doing

Before touching the design we looked at how other Canadian banks handle their
business pages. CIBC, Scotiabank and National Bank all ran roughly the same
flow: an awareness piece up top, an exploration step with the product, then
the bank's other features and benefits.

The pattern was consistent enough to trust, and it made the gap obvious. RBC
led with awareness and then dropped the user into secondary tools before
letting them explore. Everyone else got people to the product faster.

<Note>
  There was no research budget on this project, so there is no usability
  testing or analytics behind these decisions. The competitive analysis, a
  close audit of the current pages, and design judgment are what drove them.
</Note>

## The constraint that shaped everything

The hardest part was designing something modern inside RBC's existing design
system. The bank has a set visual language and a fixed palette, and the
redesign had to live inside both. Most of the work was finding how far the
system could be pushed before it stopped feeling like RBC.

Answer-engine visibility was the other real constraint. Some sections had to
stay text-heavy on purpose so the pages would still perform in search. That
gave the "cut the copy" goal a hard limit, and part of the job was deciding
section by section where scannability won and where the text had to stay.

The rest of the page follows the same logic. Benefits of being an RBC business

apply, with a gradient pulled from the card art so the section ties back to

## The product pages

Same problems, different job. A product page is where someone decides, so it
has to give them enough to say yes without making them read a manual. This
structure applies to both cards we redesigned, the WestJet RBC World Elite
Mastercard for Business and the RBC Avion Visa Infinite Business Credit Card.

## Where it stands

Delivered and in development. There is no live data yet, and I am not going to
attach numbers to it that do not exist.

What I would want to watch once it ships:

- Engagement on the Apply and View Details actions
- Scroll depth, as a rough read on whether the page is genuinely more scannable
- Drop-off on the product pages, to see whether the chunked feature sections
  hold attention better than the old text wall
- Whether the business and commercial split cuts down on the wrong audience
  landing on business pages

## What I took from it

Designing something modern inside a bank's design system is its own skill. The
creativity was not in ignoring the constraints, it was in finding room to move
inside them.

Balancing that against answer-engine visibility, where some text has to stay,
meant almost every section was a tradeoff between how clean it could look and
how much it needed to say.

And working from a stakeholder ask with no research budget pushed me to lean
on competitive analysis and craft, and to be clear about what that evidence
can and cannot claim.
